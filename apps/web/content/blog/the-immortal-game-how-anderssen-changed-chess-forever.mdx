In London in 1851, Adolf Anderssen and Lionel Kieseritzky played a casual game during a break in the first great international chess tournament. The result was not important to the tournament table. The ideas on the board were important enough that players still call it **the Immortal Game**.

The game is famous for its final combination: Anderssen gives up both rooks and then his queen before delivering mate with three minor pieces. That summary is accurate, but it can teach the wrong lesson. Random sacrifice was not the source of the attack. Anderssen first developed pieces, opened lines, and kept Kieseritzky's king in the center. The sacrifices worked because the position was already asking for them.

## The opening creates an unbalanced game

The moves began `1.e4 e5 2.f4`, the King's Gambit. White offers the f-pawn to divert Black's e-pawn and open the f-file. Modern analysis does not treat the King's Gambit as a forced win, but the opening immediately creates practical questions: can Black finish development, and can the black queen collect material without losing time?

After `2...exf4 3.Bc4 Qh4+ 4.Kf1`, Anderssen had already surrendered castling rights. Kieseritzky then played `4...b5`, offering a pawn to push the bishop away. Anderssen accepted with `5.Bxb5`.

This is where the game becomes more useful than its legend. Both kings are vulnerable. Both players are taking material. The difference is that Anderssen's pieces soon point toward the center, while Black's queen spends several moves searching for safety.

<PositionLesson title="Count moves, not just points">
  A rook is worth more than a bishop, but a rook asleep in the corner
  contributes nothing to an attack. Before accepting material, count how many
  moves your opponent gains for development and how many moves your queen will
  need to escape.
</PositionLesson>

## Development supplies the attack

The critical sequence continued with `5...Nf6 6.Nf3 Qh6 7.d3 Nh5 8.Nh4 Qg5 9.Nf5`. Anderssen's knight reached f5, his bishop remained active, and his queen and dark-squared bishop could enter quickly. Kieseritzky's queen, meanwhile, had moved from d8 to h4, h6, g5, and later g6.

After `9...c6 10.g4 Nf6 11.Rg1`, White's rook joined the attack through the open g-file. Black replied `11...cxb5`, finally taking the bishop on b5. Materially, that looked sensible. Positionally, it did nothing about the gathering force around the black king.

Anderssen pushed `12.h4`, gaining another tempo on the queen. After `12...Qg6 13.h5 Qg5 14.Qf3`, White's pieces were coordinated and Black's kingside pieces were still at home.

The practical rule is simple: when an exposed queen is repeatedly attacked, every attack can become a developing move. The player gaining those tempi should look for open files and forcing moves before the opponent catches up.

## The sacrifices remove defenders

The game reached its famous phase after `14...Ng8 15.Bxf4 Qf6 16.Nc3 Bc5 17.Nd5`. White's knight arrived on d5 with threats, and Black grabbed a pawn with `17...Qxb2`.

Then came `18.Bd6`. Anderssen allowed `18...Bxg1`, giving up the rook on g1. After `19.e5`, the queen was attacked again, and `19...Qxa1+` removed the second rook.

If you stop here and count, White appears to have been ruined. But counting attackers and defenders tells a different story:

- White's knights occupy f5 and d5.
- The bishop on d6 cuts important escape squares.
- White's queen can enter on f6.
- Black's queen is far away on a1.
- Black's king remains trapped behind undeveloped pieces.

Anderssen did not ignore material. He recognized that Black had no time to use it.

## The final combination is forcing

After `20.Ke2 Na6`, Anderssen began a sequence in which every move carried a direct threat:

1. `21.Nxg7+` forces the king to d8.
2. `22.Qf6+` offers the queen and forces `22...Nxf6`.
3. `23.Be7#` ends the game.

The final position is the image that made the game immortal. A bishop on e7 and knights on g7 and d5 coordinate the mate. White's queen and rooks are gone, yet Black's material advantage cannot answer a checkmate.

<PositionLesson title="A sacrifice needs a concrete job">
  The rook sacrifices dragged Black's queen away while White's minor pieces
  occupied mating squares. The queen sacrifice removed the knight from g8's
  defensive role. Each sacrifice changed a specific feature of the position;
  none was made for atmosphere.
</PositionLesson>

## What to borrow for your own games

You do not need to play nineteenth-century gambits to use Anderssen's ideas.

### Develop with threats

A developing move becomes more valuable when it attacks the queen, creates a pin, or claims an open file. Look for moves that improve your position and reduce your opponent's time at once.

### Compare local force before total material

An attack happens in one part of the board. Count the pieces that can actually reach the king, not every piece still listed on the scoresheet.

### Open lines while the king is uncastled

Central pawn breaks and rook files are strongest before the defender connects the rooks and brings pieces back. If the king is safe, a sacrifice may simply be material lost. If the king has no shelter, time can be worth more.

### Calculate to a stable result

Anderssen's final sequence works because it ends in mate. When considering a sacrifice, calculate until you reach something stable: checkmate, perpetual check, recovered material, or a clearly favorable position. “It looks dangerous” is a reason to calculate, not a conclusion.

## A masterpiece, not a template

The Immortal Game belongs to the Romantic era, when open games and direct attacks were celebrated. It should not be treated as proof that development always justifies a queen sacrifice. It is better understood as an unusually clear demonstration of coordination: all of White's active pieces participated, while Black's extra material remained too far away.

You can review the complete score in the [Chessgames game record](https://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1018910), then set up the final position yourself. Do not begin by asking whether you would have found `22.Qf6+`. Begin earlier: notice how many tempi Black's queen spent, and how every white piece found a useful square.

That is the part of the Immortal Game a modern player can repeat.
