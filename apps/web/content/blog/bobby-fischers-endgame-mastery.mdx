Bobby Fischer's endgames are memorable because they often feel inevitable in retrospect. A small edge becomes a fixed weakness. The king walks toward the center. An apparently active enemy piece loses its useful squares. Only then does the material balance change.

That smooth finish can hide the hard part. Fischer was not winning merely because he “knew endgames.” He repeatedly made accurate decisions about activity, pawn structure, and which pieces should remain on the board.

Here are four habits worth looking for when you replay his games.

## 1. Improve activity before collecting pawns

In an endgame, a piece's value depends heavily on where it can operate. A rook trapped behind its own pawns can be less useful than an enemy rook controlling an open file, even when the material count is equal.

Fischer's pieces were often active before he tried to win a pawn. His rooks entered open files, his bishops claimed diagonals on both wings, and his king moved toward the relevant sector of the board.

Use this comparison when deciding whether to capture:

- Where will the capturing piece stand afterward?
- Does the capture open a file for the opponent?
- Can the opponent gain a tempo by attacking the piece?
- Would an improving move make the same pawn easier to win later?

Greed is especially expensive in rook endings. Taking a pawn can place the rook in front of its own passed pawn or allow the enemy rook behind it. Activity frequently matters more than the first pawn you can see.

  Before calculating a pawn grab, identify your least active piece and its best
  square. If you can improve it without allowing counterplay, the position may
  become easier to calculate on the next move.
</PositionLesson>

## 2. Treat the king as a working piece

The king changes role when queens and enough attacking pieces leave the board. Instead of hiding, it can defend pawns, attack weaknesses, escort a passer, and deny entry squares to the enemy king.

Fischer was particularly good at recognizing the moment when king activity became safe. This is not the same as marching the king forward automatically. A rook and minor piece can still create mating threats, and a king can be cut off by an enemy rook.

Before activating your king, check:

1. Can the opponent give a dangerous series of checks?
2. Which entry square does the king want?
3. Can an enemy pawn fix or block that route?
4. Will a piece trade make the king's journey safer?

Once the route is safe, king activity often creates a second front. The opponent must defend against both piece penetration and the king's approach.

## 3. Fix a weakness before attacking it

A weakness is only useful if it cannot easily move or be exchanged. Fischer frequently restrained a pawn before increasing pressure on it. That gave the defending pieces fewer active choices.

Suppose a backward pawn can advance and trade itself for a healthy pawn. Attacking it immediately may accomplish nothing. First control its advance square. Then place a piece where it attacks the pawn without allowing a freeing break. Only after the weakness is fixed should additional pieces pile on.

This sequence—**restrain, blockade, attack**—makes many technical endings easier to understand.

Pawn moves are permanent, so the defender also has to be careful. Pushing a pawn out of attack may create a weak square behind it. Fischer's patient play often forced opponents to choose which long-term concession they preferred.

## 4. Preserve the right imbalance

Trading pieces does not automatically make a winning position easier. If your advantage is a superior bishop, exchanging that bishop may remove the very reason the ending favors you. If your opponent has the more active rook, exchanging rooks may be exactly what you want.

One famous study is Fischer's fourth match game against Mark Taimanov in Vancouver in 1971. The ending is usually discussed as a demonstration of bishop-versus-knight play. Fischer kept the bishop active across both wings, restricted the knight, and used patient king and pawn improvements. The lesson is not that a bishop always beats a knight; it is that a long-range bishop becomes stronger when there are targets on both sides and the knight lacks stable outposts.

When considering an exchange, ask what remains:

- Which pawn weaknesses survive?
- Which piece can attack both wings?
- Does the exchange improve or worsen the king position?
- Whose passed pawn becomes more dangerous?
- Is the resulting pawn ending actually won?

<PositionLesson title="Do not simplify the evidence away">
  If one piece is clearly better than its counterpart, that imbalance may be
  your winning asset. Exchange pieces that obstruct it, not the piece that
  proves your advantage.
</PositionLesson>

## A method for studying Fischer's endings

Passive replay is not enough. Use a repeatable process:

### Stop at the transition

Find the point where queens came off or the game clearly entered an ending. Write down the material, pawn weaknesses, open files, and best piece for each side.

### Guess the quiet moves

Pause before every non-forcing Fischer move. Captures and checks are easier to notice; improving moves reveal the plan. Choose a move and explain what it improves before seeing the game continuation.

### Mark moments of irreversible change

Record every pawn move, exchange, and king entry. Those moves change the structure permanently and are often more instructive than a late tactical finish.

### Test the defender's counterplay

For every winning plan, identify the defender's most active resource. Could a rook become active? Could a pawn break create a passer? Fischer's technique was effective because it limited counterplay while improving his own position.

## Precision is built from small questions

It is tempting to summarize Fischer's technique as perfect calculation. His games are more useful when broken into smaller decisions: Which piece needs activity? Which pawn can be fixed? Is the king ready to enter? Which exchange preserves the advantage?

Those questions are available in your own games, even when the position is equal and no brilliant combination exists. Technical strength begins with noticing what can be improved safely and refusing to rush the position before it is ready.

For deeper study, Fischer's own _My 60 Memorable Games_ remains valuable because his annotations explain decisions rather than merely listing moves. Pair annotated games with a board, stop before each quiet improvement, and make yourself name the job that move performs.
