Garry Kasparov had White and Anatoly Karpov had Black in this rapid game from Valencia, preserved in the [Chess.com game record](https://www.chess.com/games/view/13038980). For a long time it is not a sacrificial masterpiece at all. It begins as a controlled Queen's Gambit structure, with White taking a modest central pull and Black answering with compact, practical development. The game only becomes explosive after both players have spent many moves arranging apparently small details: a queen on d2, a rook on d5, a weakened h-pawn, and a black knight that seems to gain a tempo but actually opens the door to a forcing attack.

White starts with principled central space, then challenges Black's d-pawn and develops the queen's knight before clarifying the center. The exchange on d5 is not a pawn-winning operation. It is a structural choice: White gives up the c-pawn tension and aims for an Exchange Queen's Gambit shape where the bishop can come out actively and the pieces can develop without friction.

## Why the Game Ended
The recorded final position is not checkmate, not stalemate, and not a board draw. The verified ending context says Anatoly Karpov lost on time after the final recorded move, so this was a time forfeit by Black, with Garry Kasparov winning. Contemporary live coverage reported that result in [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/news/blog/2009/sep/22/kasparov-karpov-rematch).

The final board position itself is still decisive for White, but that is different from saying it is mate. White is not giving check in the final position, and the side to move is White. The concrete reason Black's position is strategically lost is that White's queen and rook still dominate the exposed king, while Black's last move only created a flight square. The verified continuation <MoveSequencePreview fen={"1q2r1k1/r4p2/1p6/p6R/4n2Q/P7/1P3PPP/5RK1 b - - 4 28"} moves={["f7f5","h5h8","g8f7","h4h5","f7e7","h8h7","e7d8","f1d1"]}>28...f5 29.Rh8+ Kf7 30.Qh5+ Ke7 31.Rh7+ Kd8 32.Rd1+</MoveSequencePreview> shows White continuing with forcing checks and rook pressure rather than claiming an immediate checkmate on the board. So the result combines two facts: Karpov's clock expired, and the final position also left Kasparov with a winning queen-and-rook attack.
