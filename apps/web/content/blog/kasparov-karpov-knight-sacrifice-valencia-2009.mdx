Garry Kasparov played White, and Anatoly Karpov played Black, in the [Chess.com game record](https://www.chess.com/games/view/13038980) from the 2009 Rapid Match in Valencia. The result was 1-0, but the route there was not a straight tactical crush. It began as a quiet Queen's Gambit Declined Exchange structure, became a central pressure game, and only then exploded when Black moved the wrong defensive knight.

## Opening Structure

The early moves set up a familiar Exchange Queen's Gambit: White fixed the d-pawn structure, Black accepted the symmetrical center, and the long-term question became simple: could White build pressure before Black solved the c8 bishop and found counterplay?

Black's recapture was forced by Stockfish. Declining it with a central or developing alternative allowed White to take on e6 and build too much space. The correct recapture kept the structure compact and left Black with the standard problem of activating the light-squared bishop.

White developed the bishop actively and put the queen on c2, aiming at h7 and supporting a later e-pawn break. Here Karpov chose a very human equalizing plan.

The idea is direct: challenge the active bishop before it becomes entrenched, invite an exchange, and centralize the queen. Karpov likely wanted a clean structure where the queen eyes h2 and helps support later development. Stockfish preferred keeping the bishop tension and developing the kingside knight, with the key point that Black could gain time against White's bishop instead of voluntarily trading it.

The trade itself was coherent, not a tactical mistake. The drawback was practical: once White accepted, his development became too easy. Black no longer had the tempo-gaining knight jump against the bishop, so White could move smoothly into a central setup.

This queen recapture was the only move. Other recaptures or delays left Black tactically exposed, so the queen had to come to d6 and hold the center. The price was that the queen would later need to step away from rook pressure.

The move suggests a restrained plan: keep the f-pawn flexible, avoid committing the knight to f6, and perhaps reroute toward g6 or f5. The more exact approach was to develop the queenside knight first, keeping a compact setup while preserving smoother coordination.

The issue was not tactical. It was that Black still had the c8 bishop and queenside knight waiting for jobs. White answered naturally with bishop development and later knight development, so Black's flexibility did not create an inconvenience.

The lesson from this phase is already visible: useful prophylaxis is only as good as its timing. Black improved king safety, but White was allowed to complete the central setup.

## The Decisive Defensive Error

Now the position was tense but still defensible. Black's king had weaknesses, but Stockfish still held the advantage to about half a pawn before the decisive mistake. The central question was not whether Black could find activity; it was which defensive piece could not be moved.

Karpov's knight move looks active. It challenges central squares and seems to gain time against White's dominant rook setup. The move suggests he wanted counterplay after a long passive phase. But it allowed the forcing sacrifice that defines the game.

White's target was h6 and the opened f-file. Once Black's g-pawn was forced to recapture, the queen could take the h-pawn and the rook could later enter on f5. The best defense was to centralize the knight differently, keeping control over the key entry squares and avoiding the forced pawn-capture structure.

<MoveSequencePreview fen={"1q3rk1/r2n1pp1/1p5p/p2R4/4N3/P7/1P1Q1PPP/5RK1 b - - 2 21"} moves={["d7e5","h2h3","e5g6","d5d4","a7e7","f2f4","f7f5","e4g3"]}>21...Ne5 22.h3 Ng6 23.Rd4 Re7 24.f4 f5 25.Ng3</MoveSequencePreview>

That is the practical problem in one sentence: when defending, do not move the piece that is preventing the opponent's forcing sacrifice. The move in the game allowed a 448-centipawn swing, from a difficult but playable defense into a winning attack for White.

The practical lessons are clear. In Exchange Queen's Gambit structures, do not leave the bad bishop problem unsolved for too long. When the opponent retreats a queen passively, add pressure before consolidating. And in a king defense, identify the piece that stops the sacrifice before moving it to an active square.
